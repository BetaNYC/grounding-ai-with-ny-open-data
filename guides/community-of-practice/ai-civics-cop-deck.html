The conversation we want to have

A city that cannot read its own laws without a vendor's permission is not governing itself.

What it means

Digital sovereignty is the principle that a municipality controls the infrastructure its democracy runs on: its laws, its data, its records, its services.

The precedent

In the 1840s New York decided water was public infrastructure and built the Croton Aqueduct, rather than renting access to it.

Where it stands

June 2026: we asked the Commission on Government Efficiency to write digital sovereignty into the city charter. It did not advance. That is the starting point.

Connect

The city's own Charter and Administrative Code are published through a vendor, American Legal Publishing. The records are public. The interface is rented. Every connector on slide 6 reads one of those rented interfaces and hands the public back a public one.

The COGE testimony was delivered by Jordan Shapiro on 2026-06-11. "It did not advance" is the honest status; do not soften it. The values in the AI tools are downstream of the values on this slide, which are from 2013 and older.

A question to put to the room: what in your city is public in principle and rented in practice?

So what do you do when the charter says no

Sovereignty is won by capacity, not declaration

What capacity looks like

  • Service design embedded in every agency.
  • Public-interest technologists on the public payroll.
  • Digital literacy for the people who run the government.
  • A civil society organized enough to hold the line when the charter does not.

The sovereignty test for an AI tool

  • Grounded, or guessing? Every answer comes from a live public record or does not come at all.
  • Does it show the receipt? A bill number, a contract ID, a section of law you can open.
  • Is every claim badged? Verified 2026-07-21 or Illustrative. No plausible numbers.
  • Is the code public? MIT. Read it, run it, fork it. Nothing to take on our word.
  • Does it disclose itself? Our own funding, in our own tool. And that it was built with AI, under staff review.

"AI is bad" is a reasonable place to start. An ungrounded model asked a civic question gives a confident answer nobody can check, which is one more thing rented from a vendor. The tools we build are a small piece of the fourth kind of capacity, and they only count if they pass the test on the right.

Help us flesh out AI Civics 201, so we can open-source it by the end of the year

Twenty classes exist in draft. The goal is a public, open-source curriculum for living with AI, built on four dimensions taught together: staying safe, in all its parts; play and experimentation; accountability; and regulation. Here is what exists and where the gaps are.

Four AI Civics 201 classes: Staying safe, Digital hygiene, Spotting AI fakes, What AI costs.

We need

Play & experimentation

We need

Exercises that are fun before they are useful.

Accountability

We have

Following the money, Filing a FOIL request, and the verified / illustrative badge on every claim.

We need

Cases from outside New York, and a way to grade an answer.

Regulation

We have

A charter ask that did not advance, and a lot of opinions.

We need

This is the gap. The class that teaches someone to read a rule, and to write one. It is where you are strongest and we are weakest.
